In the context of tort damages, Robert Stevens argues that substitutionary 
damages are the next-best substitute for the primary right infringed. These 
are assessed objectively as at the time of the infringement and are available 
even if there is no loss to the plaintiff or gain to the defendant. By contrast, 
consequential loss is assessed subjectively as at the time of judgment, and the 
recovery of consequential loss is subject to limitations that do not apply to the 
substitutive award, such as remoteness of loss.47
In the context of the misapplication of trust property by a trustee, Steven 
Elliott and others distinguish two types of money awards. One is an award of 
‘reparative compensation’ for loss caused by the breach of trust. Its measure 
is the difference between the beneficiary’s actual position and the position the 
beneficiary would have occupied but for the wrong. The other type of award is 
‘substitutive compensation’, which requires the balancing of the account with 
the trustee’s own money where the property cannot be returned in specie. This 
award is measured by the property’s objective value, and is available regard -
less of whether the beneficiary’s overall financial position is diminished or 
improved by the deprivation of the property.48
Each of the substitutionary theories relies primarily upon English cases in 
which a purportedly compensatory award was made in excess of the actual 
factual loss suffered by the claimant.

for the consequences of a wrong, and stated that compensation to rectify a

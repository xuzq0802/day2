There are difficulties with separating out rights and remedies in this way, as 
Austin concedes. This is because there are several instances in common law 
systems where the primary right cannot be described without reference to the 
injury and the availability of a remedy. Although there are more, two brief 
examples will be given here.
First, for some torts, damage is the gist of the cause of action—in other 
words, it is necessary to show damage in order to make out the primary duty 
itself—including for malicious falsehood, passing off, and negligence. The pri-
mary duty is defined in terms of a duty not to cause harm. Second, it has also 
been argued that the restitution of a mistaken transfer is also not susceptible 
to an Austinian analysis, because mistaken payment is not a breach of duty.23
Therefore, Austin’s definition is too narrow, as it does not cover certain 
court-ordered responses to events that are not based on wrongdoing but none-
theless give rise to a remedial response, such as unjust enrichment.
In the context of unjust enrichment, Peter Birks distinguished between 
‘wrongs’ (breaches of duty including tort, breach of contract, breach of fiduci-
ary duty, and breach of confidence) and contrasted them with causes of action 
such as unjust enrichment, which he called ‘not-wrongs’.24 In Birks’ view, the 
range of remedial responses available for courts in relation to wrongs was 
wider than that for not-wrongs; the remedial responses for ‘not-wrongs’ were 
limited to reversing the effects of the transfer.25

pre-suit grievance, usually an actual or threatened infringement of a substan -

Compensatory damages are also favoured, particularly as a remedy for 
breach of contract, by scholars who take a law-and-economics approach, but 
for very different reasons from the corrective justice scholars. Richard Posner, 
an American academic and a former judge, argues that it is societally ben -
eficial to allow a defendant to efficiently breach his contract where he could 
contract more profitably with a third party other than the claimant. 41 A rem-
edy which compels performance would deter ‘efficient breach’, but damages 
do not, because everyone is placed in the optimal position. This theory has 
proved highly influential in the United States. It has not been influential in 
other regions of the common law world. In fact, courts in Australia and New 
Zealand have unequivocally rejected efficient breach theory as a rationale for 
how the common law of contract and its remedies operate. 42 English courts 
appear to have ignored it. To a civilian reader, the lack of emphasis on pacta 
sunt servanda  (‘agreements must be kept’) exemplified by efficient breach 
theory may be startling.

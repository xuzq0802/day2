Otherwise, the substitutionary theories have not received express judicial 
approval in the common law world. Moreover, the theories have been criti -
cised; Robert Stevens’s theory in particular has been said to conflict with the 
conventional understanding of damages and the law on mitigation.52
More fundamentally, substitutionary theories have been said to fail to justify 
the award of money in response to the occurrence of a civil wrong irrespective 
of the consequences of the wrong.53 They do not explain why the consequences 
of a wrong cannot be adequately remedied by, for example, compensating loss 
caused by the wrong or stripping the wrongdoer of profit made through the 
wrong. Where a civil wrong has not generated any consequences and it is nec-
essary for a court to recognise that a wrong has occurred, this can be done 
through a declaration by the court or, where available, an award of nominal 
damages. Indeed, substitutionary theories have been said to fail to account for 
the availability of nominal damages.54
Before moving on from compensation, one final point should be made. 
While compensation is pivotal to the private law remedial landscape, Birks 
decried the ‘false monopoly of compensation’ in private law and argued that 
one should not be misled into thinking that compensatory remedies are the 
only remedies available for private law wrongs.55 The following remedies indi-
cate that there are many different functions for private law remedies.
